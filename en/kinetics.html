Chemical kinetics

How fast does a reaction happen, and why? It all comes down to one simple idea: particles colliding.

1.1Rate of reaction

The rate of reaction measures how quickly a chemical reaction takes place. It can be defined as the change in the amount of a reactant or product per unit of time.

Rate = change in amount ÷ time

What can be measured

Measurement methodApplicable when…
Volume of gas releasedA gas is produced (e.g. CO₂, H₂)

Reading graphs — the essentials

On an amount/time graph: the gradient (tangent to the curve) gives the rate at a given moment. A steep gradient = a fast reaction. The gradient decreases over time because the reactants are used up, and the curve levels off when the reaction is finished.

1.2Collision theory

Key definition

Collision theory: for a reaction to occur, the reacting particles must collide with sufficient energy AND with the correct orientation.

Key definition

Activation energy (Eₐ): the minimum energy the particles must have for a collision to lead to a chemical reaction.

Two conditions for a successful collision

1. Energy ≥ Eₐ   ·   2. Correct orientation of the molecules at the moment of collision

1.3Factors affecting the rate

🔹 Concentration

Increasing the concentration of a reactant in solution means there are more particles in the same volume. The particles are closer together, which increases the frequency of collisions. More collisions per unit of time → higher rate.

🔹 Surface area (solids)

Only the particles at the surface of a solid can collide with the particles in solution. By dividing a solid into smaller pieces (or a powder), you increase the total exposed surface area: more particles can react at the same time.

Example: a crushed effervescent tablet reacts much faster with water than a whole tablet.

🔹 Temperature

Increasing the temperature increases the average kinetic energy of the particles. The effect is twofold: the particles move faster → more frequent collisions; and a greater proportion of particles have energy ≥ Eₐ → collisions are successful more often.

🔹 Catalyst

A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction without being used up. It works by lowering the activation energy, which allows a greater proportion of particles to have enough energy to react.

Real example: copper catalyses the zinc + acid reaction by improving electron transfer between the zinc and the H⁺ ions.

Question: when is the reaction fastest? Justify your answer.

AnswerThe reaction is fastest at the start (between 0 and 30 s) because that is where the change in volume is greatest: 18 cm³ in 30 s, i.e. an average rate of 18/30 = 0.6 cm³/s. The rate then decreases because the concentration of HCl decreases.
Example 2Effect of concentration — 2 mol/L vs 0.5 mol/L HCl▾

Question: using collision theory, explain why a 2 mol/L solution of HCl reacts faster with zinc than a 0.5 mol/L solution.

AnswerIn the 2 mol/L solution there are more H⁺ ions per litre of solution. The particle density is higher, which increases the frequency of collisions between the H⁺ ions and the zinc atoms. More collisions per unit of time means a higher rate of reaction.
Example 3Catalyst and energy diagram▾

Question: on an energy diagram, sketch the effect of a catalyst and explain how it increases the rate of reaction.

AnswerThe catalyst lowers the activation energy (Eₐ). On the diagram, the peak is lower with the catalyst. Therefore a greater fraction of the particles have enough energy to react on collision, which increases the proportion of successful collisions and hence the overall rate of the reaction.
